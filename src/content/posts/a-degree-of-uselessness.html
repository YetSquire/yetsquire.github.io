A Degree of Uselessness

05/22/2026

The more you know, the more you realize you don’t know. This is a post about what a computer science degree will fail to teach an aspiring software dev, and how you can patch those blind spots.

There’s a strain of thought among entrepreneurs that higher education is unnecessary for ambitious engineers. Peter Thiel, famously, offers a fellowship under the condition recipients don’t go to college. Alex Karp did something similar. Elon Musk is vocal [a]about this as well. I’m pointing this out to highlight what I’m not arguing– I’m not arguing that you should go to college. I’m not arguing that your degree has no value. What I am pointing out is that there’s a natural gap[b] between education and experience, and if you have one and not the other, it can be hard to realize what that gap is.

I’m fortunate enough, as of the time of writing, to be only a month removed from higher education, while simultaneously having just been through several months of maintaining a startup’s codebase.

This is a post directed towards students and junior devs, basically containing advice I wish I heard in the early days of college. I don’t mean to condemn only hackathons, but in my opinion the format of a hackathon exemplifies a particular problem found in similar experiences across the panoply of what you might expect a new grad to have on their resume– internships, personal projects, and school assignments. The fundamental problem with these experiences is not that they are unhelpful. Rather, it’s that none of them teach engineers how to properly maintain code, or to review someone else’s code.

The ability to critically analyze a stranger’s logic, or your own logic from the past (in practice, also a stranger), is what enables a developer to sustainably improve a codebase beyond the shallowest additions. The nature of programming is that frequently, you’ll be able to find a solution, but the real question is whether it’s an acceptable solution– whether the tradeoffs you implemented are worth it.

I define this as a hidden weakness of hackathon-style programming because I’ve noticed that many people my age are fairly likely to have experiences with software limited to school projects and personal experiences. The average computer science student graduates with several projects under their belt, none longer than a semester, and having completed perhaps one or two short (<16 week) internships.
